Why doesn't my site show up on Google?

Most of the time, the problem is findability: Google can't discover, crawl or index the pages — or it indexes them but doesn't understand which search they serve. Having a site online doesn't guarantee it exists for the people searching.

  • Pages blocked from crawling or left out of the index (robots.txt, noindex or a missing sitemap).
  • A confusing structure: URLs, titles and navigation that don't say what each page is about.
  • Content that doesn't answer the real search of people looking for your service.
  • Few trust signals: no structured data, Google Business Profile or social proof.

The first step is finding out which of the four layers holds the blockage — that's what the diagnosis does.

My Instagram works, but nobody finds my business on Google.

Instagram shows your business to people who aren't looking yet; Google shows it to people who have already decided to look. They are different channels: one depends on you to keep posting or paying, the other depends on structure.

  • On Instagram, reach drops when you stop posting or advertising.
  • On Google, a well-structured page keeps showing up month after month.
  • People searching on Google already have intent — they're closer to hiring.
  • The two complement each other: Google captures the demand Instagram helps create.

It's easier to sell to people who are already looking than to convince someone to start looking.

Why does my competitor show up and I don't?

It's usually not luck or company size: your competitor has pages that answer the search better and technical signals that make Google's job easier. Often their site isn't even prettier — it's just better structured.

  • They have a dedicated page for the service people search for.
  • Titles and descriptions use the language of the people searching, not industry jargon.
  • The site is fast, correctly indexed and free of duplicate pages.
  • There are trust signals: reviews, a Google Business Profile and structured data.

Comparing layer by layer shows exactly where the difference lies — and what to fix first.

Having keywords on your site isn't having a strategy.

Keywords scattered through the text don't tell Google which page answers which question. Strategy means organizing the site around real search intent, with each page playing a clear role.

How do I know if my site is indexed on Google?

Indexed means Google has stored the page and can show it in search. The quickest way to check is to search site:yourdomain.com; the reliable way is Google Search Console, which shows page by page what made it in and what was left out.

  • If a site: search returns nothing, the site is probably not indexed.
  • If only a few pages appear, part of the site is blocked or was ignored.
  • Search Console explains why: noindex, an error, a duplicate page or crawled but not indexed.
  • A sitemap submitted to Search Console helps Google find every page.

Knowing what's out of the index is the first step of the findability layer.

How long does it take for my site to show up on Google?

A new site is usually discovered within days or a few weeks, but ranking well takes longer: it depends on how competitive the search is, the site's structure and the trust signals it builds up.

  • Indexing is fast when the site has a sitemap, internal links and nothing blocking crawlers.
  • Local and specific searches respond sooner than broad, competitive terms.
  • Technical fixes usually show results in the first few weeks.
  • Solid rankings come with consistency: useful content, reviews and mentions.

There's no fixed timeline, but there is a right order — and it shortens the path.

How do I find out which searches my competitor shows up for?

You can do it by searching Google for the services you sell, the way a client would, and noting who shows up. SEO tools show at scale which searches each site ranks for.

  • Search the way your client talks, not with the technical name of the service.
  • Notice which of the competitor's pages show up: service, blog or local page.
  • Look at what those pages have: clear titles, answered questions, reviews.
  • Use this as a map of the intents your site doesn't cover yet.

The goal isn't to copy the competitor — it's to cover the searches they left behind.

Can a small business show up ahead of a large one?

It can, especially in local and specific searches. Google shows whoever best answers the search — and large companies often have generic sites that don't answer concrete questions well.

  • Searches with a city, neighborhood or specific service favor specialists.

  • Direct answers to clients' questions get highlighted, including in AIs.

Size isn't a search criterion; structure and relevance are.

What is structured data and why does it matter?

Structured data is information written in a standard format, behind the page, that tells Google and AIs exactly what it contains: who the business is, what services it offers, its address, reviews and FAQ.

  • It helps Google understand the page without guessing.
  • It can produce richer results, with stars, questions and business details.
  • It makes it easier for AI assistants to cite the business with the right information.
  • Visitors don't see it, but it shapes how the site is read.

It's one of the foundations of the trust layer — invisible, but decisive.

Does my site need WhatsApp or a contact form?

It depends on how your clients prefer to talk. In Brazil, WhatsApp tends to convert better for local services; a form works better when you need information before replying. What matters is that the path is obvious.

  • A single main next step, repeated throughout the page.
  • A WhatsApp button with a pre-filled message reduces the client's effort.
  • A short form: only what's needed for the first contact.
  • Measuring where contacts come from shows which path works best.

The best channel is the one clients use without thinking twice.

Why do people leave my site so fast?

When visitors leave within a few seconds, the page usually didn't confirm they arrived in the right place: it was slow to load, didn't make clear what the business does, or didn't match what they searched for.

  • Too many pop-ups or too much information before the essentials.

The first few seconds decide whether a visit turns into a conversation — the action layer starts there.
