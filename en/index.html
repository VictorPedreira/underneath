schema

Schema (structured data) helps search engines better understand your site's content and show extra information in the results.

{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": "WebSite",
  "name": "Your Site",
  "url": "https://yoursite.com"
}

robots.txt

robots.txt tells search engines which areas of the site can be crawled and where to find the page map.

User-agent: *
Allow: /

Sitemap: https://yoursite.com/sitemap.xml
robots.txt
canonical

Canonical tags prevent duplicate content by telling search engines which version of each page is the main one.

<link rel="canonical"
  href="https://yoursite.com/" />
Canonical
meta tags

Meta tags set the title and description that appear on Google — your audience's first contact with your site.

<title>Your Site</title>
<meta name="description"
  content="What you do." />

sitemap

sitemap.xml lists every page on the site, making crawling and indexing easier for search engines.

Promotion brings people to your site. But being found is better, just like you found us.

When your site is shown

Instagram, referrals and ads that interrupt someone and ask for attention. They work — but they depend on you to keep paying or posting, and on that person still being interested in what you offer at that exact moment.

Being found is much better

Search structure makes your business appear at the exact moment someone has already decided to look for what you offer.

"It's easier to sell to someone who is already looking for what you sell than to convince someone to buy."

"My website is beautiful and modern, and my web designer already added all the keywords."

Having keywords on your site is not the same as having a site built on a search strategy. A website can be beautiful and still depend entirely on Instagram, referrals and ads for people to reach it.

The real question goes much deeper:

Which searches do those keywords actually capture?
Which search intent does each page answer?
How is Google indexing those pages today?
